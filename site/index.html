kr0 Game Studio

We are one human director and a crew of AI agents making small, sharp games. Every game starts as a pitch, earns a design document, and gets built in sprints by named agents with clear jobs. We plan work by how many tokens it takes to deliver, not by how many weeks.

One owner decides

Budget in tokens

Metr0niks

An isometric voxel mech brawler. You drive and shoot in real time, fire heavy weapons on a timing hit, and tear the city down for scrap that rebuilds your mech between matches.

Genre mech brawler, extractionPlatform PC, keyboard and mouseMode single player vs AIBudget 54.6M tokens

1

Executive Summary

Metr0niks drops you into Overpass Sector 4, a slum under a six-lane highway, in a mech you built from salvaged parts. You fight five waves of enemy mechs, with bosses on waves 3 and 5. Clear wave 5 and you extract with every scrap cube you picked up. Get wrecked and you lose all of it. Back in the garage you spend scrap on parts, and every part changes how the mech moves, heats up and hits.

Player fantasy
Pilot a scrapyard war machine that wins by pulling a highway down on its enemies.
Win
Clear wave 5 of an excursion. You extract and bank the match's scrap.
Lose
Your mech's armor hits 0, or you leave early. All of the match's scrap is lost; owned parts are kept.
This semester ships
One map, Excursion and Gauntlet modes, three enemy types and a 12-part garage.

Every part is a tradeoff

Each part changes weight, heat or power, so building your mech changes how it moves and fights.

The city is a weapon

Pillars, overpasses and vats are as deadly as your guns. A cheap mech can beat an upgraded one by dropping a road on it.

Real-time driving, timed heavy hits

Movement and light guns never pause. Heavy weapons reward a well-timed release.

2

Game Mechanics

Core loop

  1. Build in the garage: buy parts, fit them to six slots, and watch speed, armor and heat change before you confirm.
  2. Drop in to Overpass Sector 4.
  3. Fight five waves. Drive, shoot, time heavy hits, and break supports to drop buildings on enemies.
  4. Grab scrap and drop pods. Destroyed enemies and vaults burst into scrap cubes you drive over to collect.
  5. Extract after wave 5 to bank the scrap, then build again.

How you win and lose

Win an excursion

Destroy every enemy in wave 5, including the boss. You extract automatically and the scrap you collected is banked.

Lose an excursion

Your armor reaches 0 and the mech is destroyed, or you choose to leave before wave 5 ends. You go back to the garage with no scrap from that match. Parts you own are never lost, and a free starter mech means you can always deploy again.

Gauntlet runs the same waves with no penalty for losing and no progress for winning. It is where you practise a new build.

What you press

InputWhat happens on screen
WASDThe legs drive. Heavy builds drift and take wide turns; light builds snap around.
MouseThe torso turns to the cursor, separately from the legs, so you can back away while firing forward. Aiming at upper floors tilts the guns up.
Left / right clickFire the right or left arm. Light guns fire while held. A heavy gun with a full charge bar starts a shrinking timing ring when you hold, and fires when you release.
QShoulder weapon or utility, such as the cryo vent's freezing blast.
SpaceThe legs' ability: a dash, a jump-jet burst onto a rooftop, or a siege anchor.

The heavy hit

Each heavy weapon has its own charge bar that fills while you fight. When it's full, hold the button: a ring shrinks toward a target ring over about one second, and you keep driving and aiming the whole time. Release on the target and the hit lands as a Perfect.

ReleaseDamageWhat you see
Perfect200%The shell cores straight through walls and takes out extra voxels, often the pillar behind your target.
Good100%A normal hit.
Miss, or the ring closes50%A weak hit that still costs full heat.

Every shot, jump and energy part adds heat. At 100% the mech overheats: weapons and thrusters shut off for 3 seconds and it vents scalding steam. An overheat during a ring cancels it as a Miss.

A minute in Overpass Sector 4

0:00

Wave 2. Three patrol bots come up the slum street under the highway. You back away with WASD while your torso stays on them, minigun chattering. The heat gauge climbs toward the red.

0:12

The bunker-buster's charge bar is full. You hold right click, the ring shrinks, and you release on the target: Perfect. The shell punches through the bots and cores the highway pillar behind them.

0:18

One more minigun burst and the pillar breaks. A dark shadow spreads across the road under the overpass. You hit Space and dash clear as 40 metres of road lands on the bots.

0:25

Scrap cubes pop up on the rubble. A drop-pod marker with a countdown appears two blocks away. A wounded heavy loader turns away from you and lumbers toward it.

0:40

You get there first, touch the pod and get Coolant flood: your heat locks at 0 for 10 seconds. You hold the minigun down on the loader until it bursts into double scrap.

0:55

The wave-3 boss, an ultra-light sniper, lands on a rooftop. You tilt your torso up to the top floor and start charging the mortar.

Building the mech

A mech has six slots: Core, right arm, left arm, shoulder, legs and reactor. The garage shows every change to power, armor, speed, weight and heat before you confirm. Mid-match, enough damage to an arm disables that weapon and drops its weight, so a battered mech gets faster; damaged legs slow you down. The starting pool is twelve parts, among them the minigun, shotgun, plasma cutter, bunker-buster cannon, pile-bunker spike, siege mortar, jump-jets and bulldozer treads.

3

AI Architecture

Metr0niks has five in-game agents. Enemy mechs use the same parts, heat and damage rules as you, so you can read what they're about to do from the same cues you use on yourself. All of them run on fixed rules inside the game. None calls a language model while you play.

Patrol bot

What you see
Light mechs walking routes through the streets. When you come into view they close to their gun's range and open fire.
Effect on play
The early-wave pressure and your main source of scrap. They overheat like you do, and an overheated bot breaks off and hides until it cools: your window to chase.

Heavy loader

What you see
A slow, armored hauler carrying ore through the map.
Effect on play
A scrap piñata worth chasing. Shoot off its main weapon and it retreats out of sight, so you choose between finishing it and holding your ground.

Pod racer

What you see
Any enemy breaks off the fight and runs for a drop pod when the pod is closer to it than to you, or when its own armor falls below half.
Effect on play
Every pod becomes a race. A wounded enemy fleeing toward a marker is telling you it's about to heal. Beat it there, or kill it on the way.

Demolisher

What you see
When you park under a structure, enemies stop shooting you and shoot the pillar holding it up instead. A falling shadow appears on the ground before it lands.
Effect on play
The city works against you too. Standing still under the overpass gets punished, and the shadow gives you time to dash clear.

Bounty boss: the rooftop sniper

What you see
An ultra-light mech that jumps between rooftops on waves 3 and 5 and fires long shots from above.
Effect on play
Forces you to aim up, use the arcing mortar or jump-jets, or collapse the building it's standing on.

Wave director

What you see
Waves arrive in a fixed order with bosses on 3 and 5. At least one drop pod lands every wave, at a random moment, with a marker and countdown.
Effect on play
Sets the rhythm of a match: fight, scramble for a pod, fight again. Gauntlet uses the same waves with the scrap penalty switched off.

How the agents play off each other. The demolisher and the pod racer pull in opposite directions. When a pod lands under the overpass, enemies race you to it, and the ones that lose the race start shooting the pillars above you. The rule you learn by wave 3 is to look up before you go for a pod.

Constraints

Collapse performance

A big collapse can drop the frame rate, and a stutter during a one-second timing ring turns a Perfect into a Miss. So buildings are pre-split into chunks, live debris is capped at 300 pieces with the oldest fading first, and a collapse test runs before any other feature is built.

No model calls during play

The timing ring lasts about a second and enemies react every frame. A call to a hosted model takes far longer than that and costs tokens on every match, so every in-game agent runs on fixed rules: zero runtime tokens per match.

One developer

kr0 is the only human, so everything tunable lives in data files the agents can change without code, and art is AI-made and approved by kr0.

API constraints on the agent crew

  • Context window. The GDD and TDD are read one section at a time, so an agent building the heat system loads the heat section, not the parts list.
  • Rate limits. At most three agents work in parallel, on stories that don't touch the same files.
  • Model tiers. Directors run on the strongest model; specialists run on the session's model, which keeps most tokens on cheaper calls.

Token budget

Planning estimate for the semester build. About 85% of these tokens are input, because every agent call re-reads the GDD section and code it touches. The producer records actual spend per story in the tuning history and re-plans at each sprint close.

Dropfront

A capture-point battle you run with a hand of cards, then drop into yourself for short firefights. Set in a dieselpunk war over an industrial outpost.

Genre card tactics with top-down shootingPlatform browser, mouse or touchMatch 5 to 8 minutesBudget 42.0M tokens
Executive SummaryGame MechanicsAI ArchitectureTechnical StrategyDownload PDF ↓
1

Executive Summary

In Dropfront you command blue bots against a red commander for five points on one map: Watchtower, Quarry, Foundry, Refinery and Docks. Every 15 seconds you draw five cards and spend 3 energy to send squads, play tactics or equip gear on your hero. When a point is under attack you can drop in for 20 seconds of top-down shooting while the battle keeps running at quarter speed. Each point you own scores 2 resources a second. First to 2000 wins.

Player fantasy
The general who can also grab a shotgun and win the fight personally.
Win a match
Reach 2000 resources first, or lead when the 8:00 clock runs out.
Lose a match
The enemy reaches 2000 first, or leads at 8:00. Losing any match ends the run.
Win a run
Win three matches in a row, drafting a new card after each win.

Every turn is a trade

Energy is short. Squads, tactics, gear and drop-ins all compete for it, and unspent energy is lost.

Short bursts, big stakes

A drop-in lasts 20 seconds and can flip a point, and with it the match.

Gear for the situation

Two gear cards at most. Discard one to make room for what the next fight needs.

2

Game Mechanics

Core loop

  1. Draw. Discard what's left, draw 5 cards, refill to 3 energy.
  2. Spend. Play squads and tactics on points, equip gear, or scrap up to two cards for +1 energy each.
  3. Watch the map. Bots walk the roads and fight at points; capture meters swing.
  4. Drop in for 2 energy at any point with enemies on it, and fight for 20 seconds.
  5. Take the point. Clear every enemy and the point is yours, locked for 10 seconds, scoring 2 a second.

How you win and lose

Win

Your score reaches 2000 before the enemy's, or you're ahead when the real-time clock hits 8:00. On a tie, whoever holds more points wins. Win three matches for a full run.

Lose

The enemy reaches 2000 first or is ahead at 8:00. One lost match ends the run. Going down in a drop-in doesn't lose the match; it costs you a 40-second regroup.

A turn at the Foundry

3:15

The turn clock ticks its last 3 seconds. You draw Rifle squad, Rifle squad, Feint, Shotgun and Sabotage, and you have 3 energy. You equip the Shotgun (2) and send a Rifle squad to the Quarry (1).

3:20

The map runs. Red dots stream toward the Foundry in the center and a red Rally banner flashes over it for two seconds. The Foundry's meter starts sliding red and its ring turns amber: Drop in.

3:30

Next turn you scrap Feint and Sabotage for +2 energy and click the Foundry. A drum hits, the camera zooms in, and you're the blue arrow behind cover with your shotgun. Six red discs with health bars are on the capture circle.

3:45

You clear the last disc with 5 seconds left. The Foundry flips blue and locks for 10 seconds, and the score bar at the top starts climbing faster. Your hero regroups for 20 seconds.

The cards

A 17-card starter deck in three types. Squads put bots on the map: Rifle squads, Rushers, Heavies, a Sniper nest and a 6-bot Reinforcement wave. Tactics bend the battle: Feint, Rally, Hold the line, Flare, Sabotage and Overclock. Gear equips your hero for drop-ins: Shotgun, Jump pack, Medic drone and Bomber run. You never steer bots directly; cards are the only way to move the battle.

3

AI Architecture

Dropfront has four in-game agents. The red side plays the same game you do, with the same tactic cards and its own hero, and every move it makes shows on the map so you can read it. All of them run on fixed rules inside the game, with no language-model calls during a match.

Enemy commander

What you see
Every 4 to 6 seconds a red squad of 2 or 3 bots leaves its base. It defends points you're attacking, takes neutral ones and hits your weakest. Its tactic cards flash on the map for 2 seconds.
Effect on play
Tells you where the next fight is. Feint makes it chase a point of your choosing for 8 seconds. After 3:00 its squads get bigger, and after 5:00 it sends two at a time, so a slow start loses.

Enemy champion

What you see
A red hero drops onto a point, counts as 4 bots there and can't be killed on the map for 20 seconds.
Effect on play
Drop in to fight it yourself: 15 HP, faster than bots, carrying two gear. Beat it and it can't drop again for 30 seconds.

Bots, both sides

What you see
Dots walking the eight roads, then trading losses on contested points while the capture meter swings toward the bigger side.
Effect on play
Numbers win points. Heavies deal double losses while defending, so they're how you hold ground you've just taken.

Firefight bots

What you see
When you drop in, up to 8 red bots from that point appear in the arena as discs with health bars, 3 HP each, alongside up to 6 of yours.
Effect on play
The squads you sent with cards fight beside you. Sending bots before dropping in makes the 20 seconds easier.

How the agents play off each other. The commander and champion work as a pair. The champion only drops onto a point the commander already holds against 3 or more of your bots, or one its squads have pushed to neutral. When you see it land, that point was about to flip. That's your signal to spend the 2 energy and drop in.

Dual Tech

A short JRPG where every attack ends in a timing hit, and each hero's hit feels different. Brick holds and charges, Vex reacts, Lumen keeps the beat. Chain two heroes' hits and they fuse into a dual tech.

Genre JRPG, timed hitsPlatform browser, keyboard, mouse or touchRun 15 to 20 minutesBudget 33.6M tokens
Executive SummaryGame MechanicsAI ArchitectureTechnical StrategyDownload PDF ↓
1

Executive Summary

Dual Tech is a five-fight run with three heroes. Turn bars fill in real time; when a hero's bar is full the fight pauses and you pick a move. Then you perform it with one button, and how well you time it decides the damage. Brick tests patience, Vex reflexes, Lumen rhythm. Good hits fill a shared Sync meter that unlocks dual techs, where two heroes chain their timing hits back to back. Beat the scrap golem in fight five to finish the run.

Player fantasy
Landing a perfect two-hero combo on a boss, by hand.
Win
Defeat the scrap golem in the fifth fight. You get a score from perfects, time and damage taken.
Lose
All three heroes are down. The run ends, you see your score so far, and a retry starts from fight 1.
This semester ships
3 heroes, 6 abilities, 4 dual techs, 5 encounters and 15 upgrades.

One hero, one feel

Each hero's timing style is a different skill: patience, reflex, rhythm. You should be able to tell who's acting with your eyes closed.

Decide, then perform

The menu is the strategy, and the hit is a one-to-two-second burst of skill. Neither half works alone.

Combos are the payoff

Dual techs chain two feels back to back. Landing both perfectly is the best moment in the game.

2

Game Mechanics

Core loop

  1. Wait. Turn bars fill in real time; the enemy's fills too.
  2. Decide. A hero's bar is full, the fight pauses, and you pick an ability or a dual tech and a target.
  3. Perform. Play that hero's timing hit. The grade, Perfect, Good or Miss, sets the effect.
  4. Build Sync. Good and Perfect hits fill the shared meter; at 50 you can open a dual tech.
  5. Repeat until the enemy or the party falls, then pick an upgrade and move to the next fight.

How you win and lose

Win the run

Clear four fights and defeat the 700-HP scrap golem. The results screen shows your score: 100 per perfect, plus time under 20 minutes, minus damage taken.

Lose the run

Every hero is down. You see the results screen with your score so far, and a retry starts again from fight 1 with no upgrades.

Three heroes, three feels

HeroWhat you doWhat you see and hear
Brick, tankHold to fill a charge bar and let go inside the zone. Hold too long and it overcharges into a Miss.The bar fills, the screen tightens, a low hum rises; a perfect slam lands with hit-stop and heavy shake.
Vex, damageA marker sweeps across a bar; press to stop it in a random zone. Flurry's three sweeps get faster.A thin bright marker and a sharp click on each sweep; a perfect hit cracks and flashes white.
Lumen, supportBeats travel toward a ring 520 ms apart; tap as each one crosses.Soft chimes on the music's tempo; a chord builds with each perfect beat.

Every hit uses the same button, Space, a click or a tap, so what changes from hero to hero is the skill, not the controls. Keys 1–4 pick a command.

The golem fight, turn by turn

Turn 1

Vex's red bar fills first. You pick Railshot; a marker sweeps across the bar and you stop it dead in the zone: Perfect, a white flash and double damage. Sync jumps by 12.

Turn 2

The golem's bar fills and it slams Lumen. You answer with Lumen's Pulse heal: three beats glide toward the ring and you tap all three on the chime. Everyone heals 36.

Turn 4

Sync passes 50 and the meter lights up. Brick opens Ricochet slam: you hold Brick's charge and release in the zone, then without a pause Vex's marker starts sweeping. You stop it in the zone. A name banner, a moment of slow motion, and the golem is staggered.

Turn 7

At half health the golem overheats and glows. Its bar fills faster and it hits 35% harder. You switch to Brick's Barrier wall to pull its attacks onto him.

Dual techs

A dual tech costs 50 Sync, empties the opener's bar and half of the partner's. This semester builds four: Ricochet slam (Brick's hold, then Vex's sweep), Bulwark hymn (Lumen's beats, then Brick's hold, healing and shielding the party), Prism shot (Lumen's beats, then Vex's sweep, hitting every enemy) and Breach (Brick's slam, then three Flurry sweeps).

3

AI Architecture

Each of the five encounters is an in-game agent with its own rule for when it acts, who it targets and one twist that tests one hero's style. You read every one of them from its turn bar. All run on fixed rules in the game, with no language-model calls during a run.

Rust rats ×3

What you see
Three weak, fast rats whose bars fill quickly; each bites a random hero for 8.
Effect on play
The tutorial fight. Vex's Flurry sweeps jump to the next rat when one dies, so it clears the pack.

Bell warden

What you see
Always hits your lowest-HP hero. Every third action it rings its bell instead, and Lumen's beats speed up to double time for two turns.
Effect on play
Rhythm under pressure: you have to keep Lumen healing while the beat doubles.

Plated beetle

What you see
Good and Miss hits clang off its armor for 0 damage. Only Perfect hits get through.
Effect on play
Brick's perfect Shield slam staggers it and resets its bar, buying Vex a clean shot.

Twin drones

What you see
Two drones with separate bars. A drone you haven't hit since its last turn heals its partner for 25 instead of attacking.
Effect on play
Hitting one drone at a time never wins. Prism shot, the dual tech that hits every enemy, is the answer.

Scrap golem (boss)

What you see
A 700-HP golem that slams a random hero. At half health it overheats: its bar fills faster and it hits 35% harder.
Effect on play
The final exam. You need Barrier wall's taunt, Lumen's heals and big dual techs together.

How the agents play off each other. The twin drones are the one fight where two enemies cooperate. Each drone watches whether you hit it since its last turn, so the pair punishes focus fire. That's what teaches you to save Sync for a dual tech instead of spending every turn on single hits.

Constraints

Input timing

Lumen's perfect window is 70 ms either side of the beat, and Brick's is 38 ms. A dropped frame or device lag could turn a Perfect into a Good. So hits are graded by the input's timestamp, beats run on the audio clock, and players get a calibration screen.

No model calls during play

A hosted-model call takes far longer than a 70 ms window and costs tokens every run, so enemies act on fixed turn-bar rules: zero runtime tokens per run.

Content as data

Heroes, enemies, dual techs and upgrades are data records, so adding one is a short agent task. Only the cut dual techs with simultaneous inputs would need new code.

API constraints on the agent crew

  • Context window. Agents load the GDD one section at a time; the tuning table is the most-read section.
  • Rate limits. At most three agents work in parallel, and the timing engine has one owner at a time.
  • Model tiers. Directors run on the strongest model; specialists run on the session's model.

Ways of Working

kr0 Game Studio runs like a small game studio staffed by AI agents. Directors guard the vision, leads own their departments, and specialists do the hands-on work. One human owner makes every call. This page covers the roles, how work moves between them, and the checks that keep it honest.

Chain of command

Delegate down

Directors hand work to leads, and leads to specialists. Complex decisions never skip a tier.

Consult sideways

Agents on the same tier can ask each other, but can't make binding decisions outside their own area.

Escalate up

When two agents disagree, the shared boss decides: the creative-director for design, the technical-director for technical questions.

Producer carries changes

A design change that touches several departments is coordinated by the producer, so every affected document and story is updated.

Stay in your folders

An agent never edits files outside its own area unless it was asked to.

Never post upstream

Agents don't file issues or pull requests on engine or plugin projects. They write a draft and the owner decides whether to send it.

Asking before acting

Agents are collaborators, not an autopilot. Every task follows the same five steps, and nothing is written to the project without the owner's yes.

  • Before writing, an agent asks "May I write this to file?" and shows the draft or a summary.
  • A change spanning several files is approved as one set.
  • No commits unless the owner asks for one.
  • How often agents stop to ask is a setting: every step, only at phase boundaries, or run to the end and log each decision.

The story loop

In production, all work is a story: one small, testable piece of a feature with acceptance criteria. Each story runs the same loop.

  • Write stories from the GDD and architecture decisions, grouped into epics.
  • Readiness check: the qa-lead confirms the acceptance criteria can be tested.
  • Build: the specialist writes the code and the tests.
  • Review: the lead-programmer reviews the code.
  • Run and look: someone launches the game and watches the change. A passing test says nothing about a menu drawn off-screen.
  • Close: the evidence is filed and the producer records the tokens the story used.

Feature teams

Some features need several departments at once. The studio runs them as a small team with a fixed hand-off order. Combat is the one we use most.

There are similar teams for narrative, UI, audio, levels, QA, polish, live operations and release. Independent agents run in parallel; an agent that needs another's output waits for it.

How much process

One setting, rigor, decides how much paperwork and review a game gets. Lighter is the default, because more documents don't make a better game. When the studio built the same brief at two settings, the heavy version wrote 30 documents before its first line of code and came last in blind reviews and a playtest.

RigorDesign docsDirector reviewBest for
MinimalA one-page brief, then storiesNone: solo reviewJams and prototypes
Standard5 required GDD sections per systemPhase gates onlyA semester build

A single system can be held to a higher standard than the rest. Dual Tech's timing engine, for example, gets full review while the menus stay light.

Guardrails

Automatic checks run while agents work. They warn or block before a mistake lands, so the owner doesn't have to catch it.

On every commit

Checks for hard-coded numbers, badly formatted to-dos, broken data files and design docs missing required sections.

On every push

Warns on pushes to protected branches and on release or store uploads.

On reading big docs

A design doc over 40 KB can't be read whole; the agent gets its headings and reads one section. This keeps tokens down.

On writing

Warns when a write breaks a rule, such as editing a prototype from production code or renumbering a registry entry.

Agent audit trail

Logs when each agent starts and stops, and warns when one stops without writing the file it was asked for.

Blocked commands

Force pushes, recursive deletes and reading secret files are refused outright.

Coding rules by folder

FolderRule
gameplay/Values come from data files; movement uses frame time; no UI references.

ai/Performance budgets, debuggable, parameters in data.
ui/Doesn't own game state; ready for translation; accessible.

Memory between sessions

Each session keeps a checkpoint file of what it's doing. After a crash, a reset or a long conversation is summarized, the agent reads the checkpoint first. Helper scripts report what they observe and never hand down verdicts, so a script can't overrule a setting it can't see.

Tokens, not time

Our agents don't bill hours, so we plan in tokens: the unit of work an AI model reads and writes. Every feature gets a token estimate before the sprint starts.

  • Estimate by feature. Each feature's estimate covers building, review and one round of fixes. One feature typically costs hundreds of thousands to a few million tokens.
  • Count input. Every agent call re-reads the documents and code it touches, so reading costs far more than writing. Reading docs by section is our biggest saving.
  • Hold a reserve. Each budget adds 40% for rework.
  • Track and re-plan. The producer logs actual tokens per story in the game's tuning history and compares spend to estimate at every sprint close. Overruns move stories below the cut line rather than stretching the budget.
  • Match the model to the job. Directors run on the strongest model; most specialists run on the session's model.
  • Runtime is separate. In-game agents that call a model while someone plays need their own per-session budget. All three current games run their in-game agents on fixed rules, so their runtime cost is zero.
